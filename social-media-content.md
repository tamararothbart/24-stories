### POST 2 — How it works
*Product explanation, emotionally led.*

Here's how it works.

Every week for six months, someone you love receives a prompt. Not "write about your childhood." Something specific. Something that opens a door.

*What did your hands do for a living?*
*What song takes you somewhere you can never quite explain?*
*Tell me about someone who changed the direction of your life.*

They speak into their phone or they type. A few sentences or a few pages — whatever comes.

I read every story. I tidy the edges — never change their voice, never correct their way of seeing it. Then it goes to the whole family that same week. Children in Cape Town. Grandchildren in London. A daughter who's been meaning to ask for years.

Six months later, 26 stories become a real hardcover book. Printed. Bound. Delivered to your door.

### POST 6 — The "imagine" story
*Most shareable post. Fictional but emotionally true. No product explanation needed.*

Imagine your father is 74. He worked in the same hardware shop in Germiston for forty-one years. You think you know everything about him.

Then comes Week 9. The prompt is about work.

He speaks for twelve minutes. He talks about the morning in 1987 when he arrived and found the owner had fled the country overnight. No note. No wages owed. Just a locked door and a key under the mat.

He was 35. He had three children. He took the key.

He ran that shop for the next six months on credit, on goodwill, and on the kind of stubbornness he never once mentioned to you.

You didn't know any of this.

Neither did he know that you needed to.

### LINKEDIN POST 2 — Institutional memory
*Post the following week. Thought leadership. Does not pitch the product directly.*

Most companies know they should capture their story.

The founding moment. The early culture. The decisions that shaped everything before it was called anything.

Some do it well. And what they discover every time is that the important stories are never the big ones. Not the funding round or the acquisition. The argument in a garage that saved the company. The client who said no and why. The person everyone underestimated.

The details. The texture. The almost-overlooked moments that turned out to matter most.

Your family has the same story.

Not the official version — the dates, the achievements, the places lived. The real one. The decision your grandmother made the year before your mother was born. The job your grandfather never talked about. The thing someone almost said at a dinner table and didn't.

Companies invest in capturing institutional memory because they know it shapes everything that comes after.

Your family's institutional memory shapes you. Has shaped you. More than any job you've ever had, any company you've ever worked for.

It just hasn't been asked for yet.
